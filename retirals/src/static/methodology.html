🚀 Getting started

Getting started

The default inputs show a realistic mid-career scenario: age 35, planning to retire at 60, with ₹15 lakh annual expenses, ₹40 lakh current corpus, ₹5 lakh annual contribution growing at 8%, and a 60/30/10 equity-debt-arbitrage allocation. This is a plausible starting point for many Indian professionals, but it is intentionally set slightly below full readiness so you can see how the tool identifies gaps and recommends actions.

Enter your current age, retirement age, life expectancy, current corpus, annual expenses, annual contribution, return assumptions, and inflation. Add one-time expenses for known future costs such as education, housing, or medical care.

The planner also supports a retirement lump sum, pension income, investment allocation, withdrawal tax assumptions, and market-stress scenarios. Use the deterministic simulation for one assumed return path; use Monte Carlo to assess many possible market paths.

Amounts are treated as annual values in Indian rupees. Check that all assumptions use the same time frame and that future expenses are expressed in today's money when entering them.

📊 Deterministic projection

Deterministic projection

The retirement projection applies the selected pre- and post-retirement return each year. It adds contributions before retirement, inflates expenses, applies pension income and withdrawal taxes, then updates the corpus. It is useful for understanding the cash-flow mechanics of one scenario, but it does not show the range of possible market outcomes.

Stress scenarios

Mild and severe stress scenarios replace the first two retirement-year returns with fixed negative returns. They are designed to show the effect of a poor start to retirement.

Pension and tax treatment

Net pension first funds recurring expenses. A pension surplus is reinvested only when that option is selected. Withdrawal taxes use a simplified blended allocation approach and an annual LTCG exemption; they do not track individual tax lots or cost basis.

🎲 Monte Carlo simulation

Monte Carlo simulation

Monte Carlo runs 100 to 50,000 simulated annual-return paths. Every path follows the same contributions, expenses, pension, tax, and withdrawal rules, but its market returns differ. The success rate is the share of paths that fund all required expenses through life expectancy.

The simulation uses either a normal or lognormal return distribution. Lognormal is the default because it cannot produce a return below −100%. A random seed makes a run reproducible; leaving it blank produces a new random set of paths.

Allocation and volatility

Equity, debt, and arbitrage weights, volatilities, and correlations are combined into one covariance-based portfolio volatility. The current model draws one return for the total portfolio; it does not simulate separate asset-class balances or rebalancing.

✅ Best practices

Best practices

  • Use real returns when possible: If you expect 10% nominal returns and 6% inflation, your real return is roughly 4%. The planner inflates expenses automatically, so nominal returns are usually easier to reason about.
  • Test stress scenarios early: Run Normal, Mild Crash, and Severe Crash before locking in your plan. If a mild crash breaks your plan, you are running too close to the edge.
  • Keep simulations realistic: 1,000–5,000 simulations are usually enough for stable results. More simulations reduce noise but do not remove model uncertainty.
  • Update annually: Re-run the planner every year with actual values. Assumptions drift, salaries change, and markets move.
  • Separate known and unknown expenses: Use ad-hoc expenses for known future costs (education, house, medical) and keep regular expenses in the main expense field.
  • Check the funding probability curve: The final success rate is a summary. The year-by-year funding probability shows exactly when your plan becomes fragile.
  • Use sensitivity analysis: Try retiring 1–3 years earlier and later. A small change in retirement age often has a large impact on success probability.
❓ Frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

What return rate should I use — 6%, 8%, 10%?

Use a return rate you believe is realistic for your portfolio mix over the long run. Historical Indian equity returns are around 10–12% nominal, but future returns may be lower. A conservative blended assumption of 8–10% pre-retirement and 7–9% post-retirement is common. The planner's default is 10% pre and 8% post.

Why is my readiness below 100%? Does that mean I cannot retire?

Readiness below 100% means your projected corpus is below the deterministic minimum required. It does not mean retirement is impossible — it means the plan is fragile and likely to fail under adverse markets. Use Monte Carlo to see the actual success probability and identify specific actions to close the gap.

What is a good Monte Carlo success rate?

There is no universal threshold. Many planners target 80–90% for a moderate risk tolerance and 90–95% for a conservative one. If your success rate is below 70%, the plan is fragile. Below 50%, it is high risk. Consider your ability to reduce expenses, delay retirement, or increase savings before settling on a lower threshold.

How much should I save each year?

There is no fixed percentage, but a common starting point is 15–20% of income for Indian professionals in their 30s and 40s. The planner's default of ₹5L on a ₹15L expense base is about 33% of expenses, which is aggressive but achievable for many. Use the recommendations to estimate the exact additional amount needed for your situation.

Should I include a pension?

Include it only if you are certain of the amount and timing. Government and corporate pensions are relatively predictable in India, but private pensions vary. The planner assumes pension grows with inflation and is taxed at the rate you enter. If you are unsure, model both with and without pension to see the impact.

What are LTCG and STCG?

LTCG = Long-Term Capital Gains (taxed at 12.5% for Indian equities above ₹1.25L). STCG = Short-Term Capital Gains (taxed at 20% for Indian equities). Debt fund gains are taxed as per your income slab after 3 years. The planner blends these based on your equity/debt/arbitrage allocation and equity LTCG/STCG split.

How does the withdrawal tax work?

The planner estimates tax each year by blending your asset-class tax rates according to your allocation. It also applies the annual LTCG exemption. This is a simplification — it does not track individual cost basis or tax lots — but it is sufficient for projection purposes.

Why does the planner show a gap even when I have a large corpus?

A large corpus can still fall short if expenses are high, inflation is long, returns are lower than expected, or you live longer than planned. The planner compares your corpus against the minimum required to fund your specific expense and longevity assumptions. A gap means the plan is sensitive to bad luck or assumptions.

📖 Glossary

Glossary

Corpus
The total investible wealth available to fund retirement. It grows with returns and contributions, and shrinks with withdrawals and expenses.
Readiness
Deterministic coverage ratio: projected corpus at retirement divided by minimum corpus required. 100% means the deterministic projection breaks even.
Success rate
In Monte Carlo, the percentage of simulated paths where expenses are fully funded through life expectancy.
Blended tax rate
A weighted average of LTCG, STCG, debt, and arbitrage tax rates based on your portfolio allocation and equity split.
Annuity due
A payment stream where cash flows occur at the beginning of each period. In this planner, expenses are treated as an annuity due because they are incurred throughout the year.
Sequence-of-returns risk
The danger that poor market returns in the early retirement years permanently reduce a portfolio's ability to recover, even if later returns are strong.
LTCG exemption
The annual amount of long-term capital gains that is tax-free. For Indian equities, it is ₹1.25 lakh per year.
Ad-hoc expense
A one-time future cost entered by age, such as a house down payment, medical procedure, or child's education.
Monte Carlo simulation
A method that runs many random return paths to estimate the range of possible outcomes, rather than a single deterministic path.
Percentile bands
In Monte Carlo output, the 5th, 25th, 50th, 75th, and 95th percentile corpus values at each age, showing the spread of outcomes.

Assumptions and limitations

  • Returns are independently sampled each year; the model does not represent momentum, mean reversion, or changing market regimes.
  • Inflation, pension growth, and tax rates remain fixed at the values entered.
  • Monte Carlo paths model sequence-of-returns risk because withdrawals continue while return order varies.
  • Asset-class paths, rebalancing, changing allocations, individual tax lots, and changes in spending or health costs are not separately modelled.
  • Correlation inputs must be realistic as a group. Some individually valid combinations are not mathematically valid together and can understate calculated risk.
  • More simulations reduce random sampling noise but do not make uncertain assumptions certain.
ℹ️ About

About

Dynamic Retirement Corpus Planner is built to help individuals and families in India plan for a financially secure retirement. It combines deterministic projection with Monte Carlo simulation to answer two core questions:

  • Will my current savings and investments be enough?
  • What is the range of possible outcomes, not just a single estimate?

The tool models contributions, expenses, inflation, pension income, ad-hoc costs, withdrawal taxes, and market stress scenarios. It is designed for Indian investors and reflects common asset classes, tax rules, and retirement income sources.

This is a planning and education tool. It does not provide personalised financial, tax, or investment advice. Always review major decisions with a qualified professional.
